# Step 17C — Why the Step 17B benchmark saturated

Step 17B's human judgments were not wrong or careless — they were consistent with the rubric as given (`2 = clearly relevant`), applied to a benchmark whose queries mostly had exactly one plausible "aboutness" bucket in a 93-item, single-domain (AI/ML news) corpus. The old rubric offered only one way to say "yes, this is about what I asked" and no way to say "this is on-topic but not what I actually meant." That produced three collapsed cases:

- **Topical association** — a result shares the corpus's ambient subject (AI) with the query but does not address the query's specific ask (e.g. a coding-agent repo showing up for a query about "customer service chatbots"). Step 17B's rubric had no score for this — it was either "relevant" (2) or invisible (never returned). Nothing forced a judge to notice and penalize a same-topic-wrong-target result.
- **Somewhat useful search result** — a result that is a real, defensible partial answer (adjacent subtopic, secondary angle, broader category than asked) but not the direct thing searched for. Step 17B had no `1` tier to place this in.
- **Strong/direct answer** — a result that is exactly what the query asked for. This is the only case Step 17B's `2` was ever really testing, and per your confirmation, that's genuinely what you rated everything as.

Given the old benchmark's queries mostly resolved to "does *anything* in this narrow AI corpus loosely relate," and lexical FTS's AND-semantics made it return **zero** results for most multi-word conceptual phrasing (so there was rarely a losing alternative to compare against), the evaluation measured **coverage** (did a method return anything at all) rather than **discrimination** (is what it returned actually the right answer, or just topically nearby). Step 17C's new rubric (0/1/2 with an explicit "not useful even if generally AI-related" `0`) and its benchmark design (queries with a defensible single correct target, plus explicit ambiguous/negative-control queries) are built specifically to force that distinction to show up in the data.
